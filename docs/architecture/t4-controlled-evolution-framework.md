# T4 — Controlled Evolution Framework

> Generalized — institutional WaaS architecture corpus, stewardship practice for evolution under constraint.
> All generalized reasoning is ★ Hypothesis.
> No unrestricted extension. No frontier promotion without scrutiny. No revision velocity claims.

---

## 0. Why evolution needs a stewardship framework

R5 (Evolution Governance Model) specifies the **rules** for corpus change — C0-C8 classification, cooling-off periods, audit trails, stewardship roles. T4 specifies how a steward **practices** controlled evolution under those rules — what they say yes to, what they decline, what they defer, and on what grounds.

The distinction matters because evolution is **the most pressured** stewardship activity. Stewards receive proposals continuously: from authors, from readers, from AI assistance, from regulatory shifts, from frontier developments. Saying yes feels productive; saying no or "defer" feels like obstruction. Without an explicit framework for what counts as healthy evolution, stewardship drifts toward **proposal acceptance velocity** as an implicit metric — and proposal velocity is one of the most reliable signals of corpus degradation.

T4 makes the framework explicit.

---

## 1. Core thesis

> Healthy institutional theories evolve through constrained adaptation, not unrestricted expansion.
> A steward's most important word is "not yet."
> Survivability is preserved by the changes a corpus declines, not only by the changes it accepts.

## 3. The four evolution categories

Stewards distinguish four categories of evolutionary pressure (★ Hypothesis):

| Category | Source | Default response |
|----------|--------|------------------|
| **EV1 — Reality update** | External world changed (regulatory, incident-driven, technology shift) | Investigate; respond proportionally |
| **EV2 — Internal refinement** | A corpus claim has been found inadequate; refinement proposed | Accept with cooling-off; preserve prior position |
| **EV3 — Frontier expansion** | A new domain wants representation in the corpus | Defer; require E4 institutionalization stages |
| **EV4 — Reader-driven** | Readers request coverage of adjacent topics | Triage; most requests do not warrant new content |

The four categories require **different practice patterns**. A steward who treats all proposals identically is over-accepting EV3 / EV4 and under-investing in EV1 / EV2.

---

## 4. EV1 — Reality update practice

External reality changes. The corpus must respond. The discipline is to respond **proportionally**.

★ Hypothesis practice:

- **Confirm the reality update.** Is the regulatory change actually in force? Is the incident actually relevant? Is the technology shift institutionalized or speculative? Verify before responding.
- **Determine the scope.** Does the update affect one doc, one cluster, or the corpus broadly?
- **Choose the response class** (R5):
  - Single-doc clarification → C1.
  - Doc amendment with substantive position change → C3 + R7 snapshot of prior position.
  - Cluster-wide implications → C3 / C4 across multiple docs; coordinated rollout.
  - Charter implications (e.g., new regulatory regime invalidating corpus framing) → C8.
- **Avoid over-response.** Not every regulatory bulletin warrants a corpus amendment. Most don't.
- **Avoid under-response.** A regulatory change that genuinely invalidates a corpus position must produce an amendment within reasonable cycle time.

★ Hypothesis: EV1 responses are the **most justified** evolutionary pressure — and the easiest to over-process. Stewards should track EV1-driven amendments per quarter; trend lines reveal whether response calibration is stable.

---

## 5. EV2 — Internal refinement practice

The corpus's own reasoning reveals inadequacy. A reader, an AI sampling exercise, a cross-cluster review, or steward observation surfaces a refinement candidate.

★ Hypothesis practice:

- **Confirm the inadequacy.** Is the current claim actually wrong, or is the proposed refinement reflecting reviewer preference? Steelman the current claim before accepting the refinement.
- **Classify by R5.** C1 / C2 for clarification-level; C3 for position revision; C4 for supersession; C7 if ontology-touching.
- **Preserve the prior position.** R7 snapshot before refinement.
- **Document the refinement rationale.** Future stewards must understand why the refinement was accepted.
- **Defer ambiguous refinements.** If steward consensus is not strong, defer to next cycle rather than fast-tracking.

★ Hypothesis: EV2 refinements should average **modest velocity** (★ a few per cluster per quarter). High velocity suggests refinement-as-authorship; low velocity suggests under-engagement.

---

## 6. EV3 — Frontier expansion practice

Frontier domains seek entry to the corpus — CBDC, AI-assisted governance, intent-based settlement, post-quantum, autonomous treasury, new emerging primitives.

E4 (Frontier Integration Discipline) defines the **5-stage institutionalization ladder**. T4 specifies the practice:

1. **Observation stage.** Steward observes the frontier domain; tracks external literature; does not write corpus content.
2. **Hypothesis stage.** Frontier domain enters C6 (open questions) as ★ Hypothesis exploration; no canonical claims.
3. **Speculation stage.** Frontier doc may exist as Frontier cluster member (D27-D32 pattern), explicitly marked ★ throughout; not retrieved for non-speculation queries.
4. **Stabilization stage.** Domain has matured externally; corpus position can be drafted with some confidence; still ★ on load-bearing claims.
5. **Institutionalization stage.** Domain is institutionalized externally and internally; ★ markers can be selectively retired with R5 governance review; cluster membership may shift.

★ Hypothesis: most stewardship pressure on frontier domains is to **skip stages 1-3** and go directly to canonical claims. The discipline is to refuse — frontier integration is **slow by design**.

A steward asked to write canonical content on an emerging domain says: "We can write speculation-stage content. Canonical positions require evidence we do not yet have."

## 7. EV4 — Reader-driven practice

Readers request coverage of adjacent topics. Most requests do **not** warrant new content.

★ Hypothesis practice:

- **Read the request fully.** Often "we should cover X" is actually "I want a quick answer to question Y" that retrieval can already address.
- **Check existing coverage.** Is X already addressed in a cluster, just not surfaced by reading paths? If so, the response is a C5 reading-path update, not new content.
- **Check the corpus's stated scope** (C1, C5). Does X fall within scope, or has the reader's expectation drifted?
- **Triage by request frequency.** A single request rarely warrants new content. Multiple independent requests over a sustained period may indicate genuine coverage gap.
- **Default to "not yet."** A reader-driven content addition is reversed only after months of accumulated signal, not on first ask.

★ Hypothesis: ≥ 80% of reader-driven proposals should be answered with existing content, reading-path improvements, or polite deferral. The remaining ≤ 20% warrant new content — and even then, with R5 C5 cooling-off.

---

## 8. The "not yet" practice

A steward's most-used response should be **"not yet"** — not "yes" and not "no."

Why "not yet":

- It acknowledges the proposal as potentially valid.
- It preserves the option to revisit.
- It allows the steward to gather signal across cycles before committing.
- It prevents the velocity drift that comes from binary accept / reject decisions.

★ Hypothesis practice patterns:

- **"Not yet, observe for one cycle"** — proposal interesting; let's see if signal accumulates.
- **"Not yet, draft as speculation"** — content can exist at ★ speculation stage; not canonical.
- **"Not yet, frame as open question"** — proposal becomes C6 entry; revisited at C6 review.
- **"Not yet, requires evidence X"** — explicit gating; proposal returns when X is available.
- **"Not yet, deferred to next cooling-off"** — proposal accepted in principle; rolling into next cycle for governance review.

The steward who can articulate **what would change the answer** to a "not yet" is practicing controlled evolution. The steward who says "no" without articulating that condition is closing inquiry.

---

## 9. Scope governance

Every doc has a **stated scope**. Scope governance is the practice of resisting silent scope expansion (T1 D5 drift species).

★ Hypothesis practice:

- **Read the doc's stated scope** in its header / introduction.
- **Compare the most recent revisions** against the stated scope. Has the content drifted toward adjacent territory?
- **If drift is observed, name it.** Either (a) revise the scope statement deliberately (R5 C3), preserving the prior scope statement, or (b) revert the drift content to its own doc / section.
- **Refuse silent scope expansion.** A doc that grows by 30% over a year often does so by absorbing adjacent territory unwarranted.

★ Hypothesis: scope discipline is **the most overlooked** evolution practice. Stewards focus on content correctness and miss the slow drift in what content is **about**.

---

## 10. Frontier containment

Frontier cluster (D27-D32) content is **contained** by explicit discipline:

- Cluster membership is **declared**; movement between Frontier and other clusters requires R5 C3.
- ★ markers in Frontier docs are **densely populated**; canonical-style assertions are flagged in review.
- Retrieval (R1) treats Frontier content differently for non-speculation queries.
- C6 (open questions) cross-references all Frontier docs.
- Reader-facing output (R2 S6) labels Frontier-sourced content explicitly.

Frontier containment **leaks** under pressure. T4 practice includes periodic containment audits:

- Quarterly: review recent retrievals where Frontier chunks surfaced for non-speculation queries; was speculation framing maintained in synthesis?
- Annually: review whether any Frontier docs have drifted toward canonical voice; flag for E4 stage re-assessment.

★ Hypothesis: containment is **not opposition** to frontier domains. It is the discipline that lets frontier exist in the corpus without contaminating canonical reasoning.

## 11. Maturity threshold model

E4 defines 5 stages of institutionalization. T4 specifies **practical maturity thresholds** stewards use:

| Stage | Threshold signal |
|-------|------------------|
| Observation | Domain has external literature; institutional interest exists |
| Hypothesis | Multiple independent external perspectives published; first conceptual framework stabilizing |
| Speculation | Corpus draft possible with ★ throughout; reader-facing positioning as speculative |
| Stabilization | External institutional adoption visible; first canonical-tier external definitions |
| Institutionalization | Regulatory recognition or industry standardization; external operational track record |

Thresholds are **observable**, not subjective. A frontier domain that does not meet a stage's threshold cannot advance — regardless of how interesting it is, how much pressure exists, or how much reader demand the corpus receives.

★ Hypothesis: threshold violations are the **most common controlled-evolution failure**. Stewards under pressure advance frontier content one stage prematurely; over a few years, multiple frontier domains have inflated maturity claims.

## 12. Conservative evolution patterns

★ Hypothesis — patterns that signal healthy controlled evolution:

- **Cooling-off respected.** Proposals wait the full R5 cooling-off period; emergency fast-tracks are rare and named.
- **Both-positions preservation.** Evolutionary changes preserve the superseded position via R7 snapshot; future readers can compare.
- **Reviewer dissent visible.** Audit trail records reviewer-by-reviewer verdicts including dissent; consensus is not assumed.
- **Reversibility documented.** Each significant evolution carries a documented path to reversal if conditions change.
- **Velocity stable across years.** Quarter-over-quarter evolution velocity is stable; sudden spikes warrant explanation.
- **Frontier ratio stable.** Frontier-content proportion of corpus does not grow rapidly; new frontier docs replace stabilized ones.

These patterns are **diagnostic**. Stewards review them as part of T4 practice — they are the visible surface of controlled evolution.

## 13. Adaptation under crisis

Some evolutionary pressure is **crisis-driven** — major incident, regulatory shock, technology disruption. Controlled evolution under crisis is harder because pressure compresses cooling-off periods and amplifies acceptance bias.

★ Hypothesis crisis practice:

- **Acknowledge the crisis.** Do not pretend the pressure is normal.
- **Identify the minimum necessary response.** What is the smallest corpus change that addresses the crisis?
- **Distinguish crisis-driven from crisis-opportunistic.** Some proposals leverage the crisis to push unrelated changes; separate these out.
- **Use abbreviated cooling-off carefully.** R5 cooling-off can be shortened for genuine crisis response; document the abbreviation explicitly.
- **Plan post-crisis review.** Crisis amendments are reviewed within ★ 6 months post-crisis to verify they remain warranted.
- **Preserve crisis worldview.** R7 snapshot of corpus state pre-crisis; readers can later understand the corpus's pre-crisis position.

★ Hypothesis: crisis evolution is the **highest-risk** controlled-evolution pattern. Stewards under pressure approve changes that, post-crisis, they would not have approved. The post-crisis review is the safeguard.

---

## 14. Anti-patterns (controlled-evolution-specific)

- **Acceptance velocity as success metric.** Stewards measure "proposals approved" as a positive signal.
- **Cooling-off erosion.** Cooling-off periods abbreviated case-by-case until they have no remaining effect.
- **Frontier promotion without threshold.** Frontier content advanced without E4 stage threshold signals.
- **Scope expansion drift.** Docs absorb adjacent territory without deliberate scope revision.
- **Reader-driven content overgrowth.** Each reader request produces new content; corpus inflates without coherence gain.
- **Crisis exploitation.** Unrelated proposals bundled into crisis response.
- **Both-positions deletion.** Evolutionary changes silently remove superseded content.
- **Reversibility avoidance.** Evolution decisions framed as irrevocable; future stewards have no path to revisit.
- **Velocity asymmetry.** New content velocity high; supersession / sunset velocity zero — corpus only grows, never compacts.
- **"Not yet" avoidance.** Stewards default to "yes" or "no"; deferral discipline absent.

## 15. Controlled evolution failure modes (steward-internal)

★ Hypothesis — specific to T4 practice:

- **F1** — Steward approves all reasonable proposals; corpus grows faster than review capacity; quality decays.
- **F2** — Steward declines all proposals; corpus stagnates; reality drift accumulates as unaddressed EV1.
- **F3** — Steward applies "not yet" without articulating conditions; proposals never revisited.
- **F4** — Steward shortcuts E4 stages for politically prominent frontier domains; institutionalization claims inflate.
- **F5** — Steward respects cooling-off in form but not substance; reviewer engagement during cooling-off is shallow.
- **F6** — Steward fails to maintain quarterly velocity awareness; trend lines unrecognized.

Remediation: explicit "not yet" practice (F1), EV1 / EV2 monitoring (F2), conditional articulation discipline (F3), E4 threshold review (F4), reviewer engagement quality audit (F5), velocity tracking (F6).

## 16. Stewardship summary (mandatory per runner)

### Stewardship reasoning
T4 frames evolution as **proportional, conditional, and reversible adaptation** under explicit constraint. The practice distinguishes four pressure categories (EV1-EV4), uses "not yet" as a default response, enforces scope discipline, contains frontier content, and applies maturity thresholds. The steward's posture is: each accepted change is the result of an explicit judgment that **survivability is preserved or improved** — never assumed.

### Failure / survivability implication
Uncontrolled evolution is **a leading cause of corpus degradation**. New content arrives faster than review can absorb; scope drifts; frontier inflates. T4 prevents this through cadence discipline and the systematic preference for declining or deferring.

### Corpus continuity implication
Evolution is **the durability test** of the corpus. A corpus that cannot evolve becomes obsolete. A corpus that evolves too freely loses coherence. T4 specifies the middle path — slow, visible, reconstructable evolution that preserves both the prior and new positions.

### Institutional memory implication
Controlled evolution produces **rich evolutionary trace** — the audit trails (R5), R7 snapshots, reviewer dissent records, and reversibility documentation that together let future stewards understand why the corpus became what it is. Uncontrolled evolution produces a corpus whose current state is uninterpretable.

### Drift / entropy implication
Evolution is one of the primary **drift vectors** (T1). Controlled evolution is the practice that converts evolutionary pressure into deliberate change rather than silent drift. Without T4, every EV3 / EV4 pressure becomes a drift signal.

### Revision governance proposal
- EV1 responses → R5 C3 / C4 / C8 per scope.
- EV2 refinements → R5 C1 / C2 / C3 with snapshot.
- EV3 frontier expansion → C6 entry → eventual C5 new doc only after E4 stage threshold met.
- EV4 reader-driven → R5 C5 only after sustained multi-source signal.
- All accepted evolution → R7 snapshot + reversibility documentation.

## 18. Closing invariant

> Evolution is not the absence of stability; it is the discipline of changing slowly enough to remain interpretable.
> A steward's job is to ask "what would change my answer" and to wait for that change before saying yes.
> Survivability is preserved by the disciplined accumulation of "not yet" — and by the occasional, well-considered "yes."

★ Hypothesis: in 20 years, the corpus's intellectual value will depend less on what it added and more on what it **declined to add** during pressure. T4 is the discipline that produces those declinations honorably.
